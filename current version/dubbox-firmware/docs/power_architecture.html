Battery power architecture

One 3.7V Li-ion cell feeding four circuits with very different needs: a digital 3.3–5V rail for the Teensy, a bursty 3.3V rail for the ESP32, a current-hungry speaker amp, and a noise-sensitive 48V phantom supply for the mic preamp. The plan below keeps them from fighting each other.

02 — One cable, power and data

Teensy's own USB port already carries both power and data — that's what the firmware's MTP drag-and-drop already runs over. Bring a single external USB-C jack out to the enclosure and split it right at the connector, and the same cable charges the battery and gives you the file transfer, with no USB hub or switch chip involved.

Charge IC input (section 01)

Feed the charge IC's regulated system output into Teensy's VIN pin, never into VUSB. Teensy 4.1 already has an onboard Schottky diode between VUSB and VIN that arbitrates automatically — plugged into USB, it runs off USB and the diode blocks any backfeed toward the host; unplugged, it runs off VIN/battery. Wiring your own 5V rail into VUSB instead would put two live 5V sources on the same net when a PC is also connected — the one thing to avoid.

03 — Per-subsystem recommendation

Teensy 4.1 + Audio Shield

Buck-boost to 5V

TPS63060 · up to 96% eff. · 2A

The cell's 3.0–4.2V range straddles 5V, so a plain boost or buck alone can't cover the whole discharge curve — needs buck-boost. Feeds the Teensy's VIN and the Audio Shield's onboard regulator directly.

ESP32

Its own 3.3V, not shared

TPS62203 · 600mA–1A capable

WiFi TX bursts pull up to ~500mA for milliseconds. Sharing Teensy's onboard 3.3V regulator risks browning out both chips on the same spike — give the ESP32 a dedicated regulator sized for the burst.

Speaker

I2S class-D amp

MAX98357A · 2.7–5.5V in · 3W @ 4Ω

The codec's headphone out can't drive a real speaker at volume. Taking I2S straight from the Teensy (bypassing the SGTL5000's analog stage entirely) is cleaner than an analog class-D fed from line-out. Runs fine straight off the battery.

Mic / 48V phantom

Isolated low-current boost

Small boost, <20mA cap. · LC filtered

Phantom current is tiny (a few mA per mic) — efficiency doesn't matter, noise does. Filter hard before the 6.8kΩ feed resistors, and run the preamp gain stage off its own clean, decoupled tap — not a rail shared with the digital boards.

*48V @ 15mA out ≈ 0.7W, which is ~200mA at 3.7V after boost losses — small in isolation, but it's the one rail where clean current matters more than the amount. Size the battery's discharge (C) rating above the 2–2.5A peak, and pick a capacity against your target runtime — a 2500–3000mAh single cell gives roughly 3–4 hours at typical draw with headroom for peaks.

05 — Things that will bite you if skipped

01

Noise into the mic path. Every switching regulator here (5V, 3.3V, 48V) radiates and conducts switching ripple. The 48V rail and the preamp are the most sensitive things in the whole system — filter the boost output hard (LC, then ideally a linear post-regulator) before it ever reaches the phantom resistors or the preamp's supply pin.

02

Star grounding. One single return path to the battery negative terminal, with the mic/phantom analog ground kept separate until that one point. Loop grounds between the digital boards and the analog front end and you'll hear it as hum or whine.

03

Real battery protection. Never wire a bare Li-ion cell straight to a board of converters. Use a protected cell, or add over-charge/over-discharge/over-current protection (e.g. a DW01+FS8205 pair) ahead of everything else.

04

Power-path charging. If you want to use the device while it's plugged in and charging, the charge IC needs to be a power-path design (like the MCP73871) — a simple linear charger without power-path forces the battery through charge/discharge cycles while you're actively drawing current, which is harder on the cell.

Component picks are a reasonable starting point, not a validated BOM — confirm exact current draw against your actual display/amp/mic choices before finalizing regulator sizing.
